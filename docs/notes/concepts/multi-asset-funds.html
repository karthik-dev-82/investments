Multi Asset Funds

Note

This page is not investment advice. Six funds over one historical window is illustrative, not exhaustive — see “What this page doesn’t cover” below for the other 21 funds in this category and why they’re not here. Past performance of the funds named is not a guarantee of future results.

The simple explanation

A Multi Asset Allocation Fund is a SEBI-defined hybrid category that must hold at least three asset classes, at least 10% each — typically equity, debt, and gold (or silver, or exchange-traded commodity derivatives), rather than the equity-and-debt-only mix a Balanced Advantage Fund runs. The equity and debt sleeves work the same way a BAF’s do; the difference is the third leg.

Gold’s role here isn’t “another return stream” so much as a genuinely different one: it tends to do well precisely when equity is under stress (a flight to safety, currency weakness, geopolitical shocks), so adding it can smooth a portfolio’s ride in a way that shifting between equity and debt alone can’t fully replicate. It’s also a tailwind that isn’t guaranteed to repeat — gold had an unusually strong run through 2023–2025, and every number on this page sits inside a window that includes it. A stretch where gold is flat or falling would tell a different story.

Everything in Choosing a Fund still applies — Direct plan, Growth option, no lock-in — and the Risk-Adjusted Metrics: Beta, Alpha, Sharpe & Capture Ratios page explains what Beta, Alpha, Sharpe, Sortino, Calmar, and the capture ratios below mean and what counts as a good or bad reading; this page doesn’t repeat those definitions.

A few things stand out:

  • ICICI Prudential and Quant beat NIFTY 50 TRI’s CAGR outright (16.5% and 15.9% vs. 12.3%) while running roughly a third of its volatility and a noticeably shallower drawdown — a much stronger showing than any Balanced Advantage fund managed over the same stretch. Quant’s low correlation (0.54, against 0.85–0.91 for the others) means its return pattern has genuinely little to do with what NIFTY 50 was doing on a given day — consistent with a fund that leans harder on the non-equity legs — which is also why its numbers deserve more skepticism about whether they repeat, not less.

  • SBI is the standout on downside protection: −17.6% max drawdown against the index’s −38.3%, and a down-capture of just 7% — in the index’s typical bad month, this fund barely moved. It did that while still matching the index’s 13.5-year CAGR almost exactly (12.3% vs. 12.3%), the same volatility-drag story told on the Risk-Adjusted Metrics: Beta, Alpha, Sharpe & Capture Ratios page for ICICI Prudential’s Balanced Advantage Fund.

  • HDFC, Axis, and UTI trail the index on raw return while taking on much less risk (beta 0.43–0.59, drawdowns 25–27% vs. 38%) — a real tradeoff, not a flaw, and the more typical outcome in this category: ICICI Prudential and Quant’s outright outperformance is the exception here, not the rule.

The Funds tab on the Index Terminal has this same table, directly below the Balanced Advantage section, using the identical method (daily returns for Beta/Alpha/Sharpe/Sortino/Correlation, monthly returns for the capture ratios, a flat 6.5% risk-free rate).

What this page doesn’t cover

The Multi Asset Allocation category has 27 funds in AMFI’s disclosure; this page covers 6. The other 21, almost all launched far more recently, don’t yet have a long enough track record for a comparison like the one above to mean much:

  • 2020 launches (~6 years of history): Tata Multi Asset Allocation Fund, Nippon India Multi Asset Allocation Fund. Motilal Oswal Multi Asset Fund also launched in 2020, but its NAV data feed appears to have stopped updating in mid-2025 — worth checking directly with the AMC before relying on it for anything.

  • 2022–2024 launches (under 4 years of history — most of the category): Baroda BNP Paribas, Aditya Birla Sun Life, WhiteOak Capital, Edelweiss, Shriram, Kotak (the current fund; an earlier Kotak fund of the same name ran 2013–2017 and is unrelated), DSP, Sundaram, Bandhan, Mirae Asset, Bank of India, HSBC, Mahindra Manulife, Quantum, Bajaj Finserv, and Union.

A 2013-vintage fund has lived through a full market cycle — 2013’s taper-tantrum selloff, 2015–16, the 2020 COVID crash, 2022’s rate-hike correction — that a 2-to-4-year-old fund simply hasn’t. None of the 21 are excluded because they’re bad funds; there just isn’t enough history yet to say anything about them that this page’s method would stand behind.
